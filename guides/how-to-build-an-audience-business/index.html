How to build an audience business from your expertise

Madalena Rugeroni · Founder of Madgrowth · ex-Google · Updated 17 September 2026 · 10 min read

To build an audience business from your expertise, pick a narrow beat you know from your career, say something specific about it on one platform where your readers already spend time, publish on a schedule you can keep for a year, and move readers onto an email list you control. Design the offer early: at small audience sizes, services and products earn far more than subscriptions or sponsorships.

The short answer
  • An audience business earns money from people who follow your work regularly, through sponsorships, paid subscriptions, community, products or services.
  • Start with one narrow beat taken from your career and one point of view on it, published on one primary platform at a fixed cadence you can sustain alongside other work.
  • Grow an email list you own by turning platform followers into subscribers, with a lead magnet, referrals and cross-promotion with other newsletters.
  • Paid subscriptions pay little at small sizes: across beehiiv, the median free-to-paid conversion rate is 0.62% and the median monthly price is $10, so 5,000 subscribers at those medians bring in about $310 a month.
  • Services, workshops and digital products are the models that work first for a small expert audience, because one sale is worth hundreds of subscriptions.

What is an audience business, and is it right for you?

An audience business is a company built on the attention and trust of a defined group of readers, who come back for what you publish and then pay you, or your sponsors, in some form.

For a tech leader with 5 to 10+ years behind them, this usually means a newsletter or media brand about one specific part of the job: B2B pricing, platform engineering, product operations in fintech, growth for developer tools. Anyone can start a newsletter. Very few people have run a pricing change across 4,000 customers, and that experience is what readers come for.

It suits people who like thinking in public and don't mind being visible. In my Builder Archetypes framework that's the Broadcaster: someone whose voice is the distribution and who enjoys the exposure. If you'd rather sell a defined leadership role to two or three companies and never post, going fractional is the closer fit.

An audience grows slowly, so it works best when a job or advisory work pays the bills for the first year.

How do you pick a beat and a point of view?

Pick the narrowest topic where you have real experience and where a specific group of people has a recurring problem, then take a clear position on how that problem should be solved. A beat is the subject you cover. A point of view is the belief that makes your coverage different from everyone else's on the same subject.

"Leadership" is a category. "Engineering management at Series A to C startups" is a beat. "Engineering managers at that stage should stay hands-on for another year" is a point of view. The beat tells readers whether it's for them. The point of view gives them a reason to pick you.

Find the beat in your career

List the problems people already ask you about, in Slack, over coffee or on calls you did as a favour. Keep the ones where you've done the work more than once and seen it go wrong. You'll have real stories and numbers to write from, and readers can tell.

Then check each candidate:

  • Depth. Can you list 50 issue topics without researching? If you run out at 15, the beat is too narrow or too far from your experience.
  • Buyers. Do the readers have budgets, or work at companies that do? Heads of growth at SaaS companies can support sponsors, services and products. Students mostly can't.
  • Staying power. Will you still care about this in three years? You'll be writing about it every week.

Write the point of view in one sentence

April Dunford's positioning work applies to media too: readers judge you against the alternatives they already have. If those are generic newsletters on your topic, your point of view is what they can't get there. Write it as a sentence someone could disagree with.

Which platform should you start on?

Start on the one platform where your target readers already spend professional time, and repurpose from there to everything else. For most B2B tech audiences that's LinkedIn plus an email newsletter, with podcasts and communities working better as a second step.

The two do different jobs. On LinkedIn, the platform decides who sees each post, which helps you reach new people fast and means your reach can change without warning. On email, you own the list and every issue lands in the inbox, but nobody discovers you there. So publish on LinkedIn to get found, and give people a reason to join your list.

  • LinkedIn suits B2B beats read by managers, founders and buyers. I built a LinkedIn audience of over 15,000 followers, and it's where I'd start again for a B2B beat.
  • A newsletter on beehiiv, Substack, Kit or similar is the asset you keep. Start it on day one, even with a tiny list.
  • A podcast is slower to grow, and good for building relationships with guests who could become clients.
  • A community needs an existing audience to seed it, so it's the hardest place to start.

Pick one primary platform and one owned channel. Spreading across five platforms usually costs you the cadence on the one that matters.

How often should you publish?

Publish at the highest cadence you could keep for 12 months during your busiest quarter, on a fixed day, in a fixed format. For most people with a job or clients, that's one newsletter issue a week and two or three posts a week on the primary platform.

Readers build a habit around a predictable issue, and that habit turns into opens, replies and eventually sales.

What keeps the cadence alive through a bad month:

  1. A fixed shape. Same day, same structure, similar length. One main idea, one example, one takeaway. Your energy goes into the idea because the format is already decided.
  2. A buffer. Keep one finished issue in reserve for the week you're sick or travelling.
  3. A topic bank. Keep a running list of questions readers ask. Replies to your issues are the best source of next month's topics.
  4. Repurposing. Each issue becomes several posts on your primary platform, each pointing back to the newsletter.

How do you grow a newsletter or audience list?

Newsletters grow through a handful of repeatable methods: converting followers on a social platform, a lead magnet, reader referrals, swaps and recommendations with other newsletters, and appearances in front of someone else's audience. Pick two, do them every week, and track new subscribers by source.

Turn followers into subscribers

Put the subscribe link everywhere the platform allows it, such as your profile and featured section, and mention the newsletter when a post touches a topic you've covered in depth.

Offer a lead magnet

A lead magnet is something useful you give away in exchange for an email address, such as a template, a benchmark or a short email course. The best ones are an upgrade to a popular piece: the spreadsheet behind the pricing post, the full question list behind the hiring post. It should also point towards what you'll sell later. The Lead Magnet playbook in The Stack designs one, including the bridge from the free piece to the paid offer.

Use referrals and cross-promotion

Ask readers to forward the issue, in one line, in every issue. Newsletter platforms have referral programmes and recommendation networks where newsletters suggest each other to new subscribers. Swaps with writers on adjacent beats work well because their readers already read about your topic.

Show up in other people's channels

Guest posts, podcast interviews, talks and co-hosted webinars put you in front of an audience that already trusts the host. For a tech leader these are often the easiest to get, because hosts want operators with real experience.

How do you make money with a newsletter?

You make money with a newsletter through five models: sponsorships, paid subscriptions, a paid community, digital products, and services such as advising, workshops or consulting. They start to work at very different audience sizes, and for an expert with a small, specific audience the higher-priced models work first.

What the data says about paid subscriptions

beehiiv's State of Paid Newsletters 2026 report, based on first-party data from thousands of publications, puts the median free-to-paid conversion rate at 0.62%, the median monthly price at $10 and the median yearly price at $100. The top 10% of technology newsletters convert 5.55%. The typical creator launched a paid tier 45 days after starting, and subscriber count doesn't meaningfully change the price.

The first dollar comes faster than people expect. beehiiv's State of Newsletters 2026 report says the median newsletter launched on the platform in 2025 earned its first dollar in 66 days. A living income takes longer, and it depends on the offer.

Why do audiences without an offer stall?

An audience without an offer stalls because readers have no next step, you can't tell whether the work pays off, and motivation fades after a year of unpaid weekly publishing. If you're good at content, it's the trap to watch for most.

It tends to go like this. Followers go up, people leave kind comments, and selling starts to feel like it might spoil the goodwill. The offer gets pushed to "when the audience is bigger". A year later there are 8,000 followers, no revenue and no idea what anyone would pay for.

  • Decide the first offer before issue one. It can change later. Knowing it shapes what you write and who you write for. If you don't know what to sell yet, read what to sell after a career in tech.
  • Mention it from the start. A one-line footer ("I run pricing workshops for Series A SaaS teams, reply if that's you") teaches readers that you sell something.
  • Treat replies as research. When readers ask for help with the same problem more than once, you've found a product. Bob Moesta's jobs-to-be-done interviews help you find out what made them ask.

How do you start a media company alongside a job?

Start a media company alongside a job by running it as a weekly publishing habit for the first six months, with one beat, one platform, one newsletter and one offer, and add people or formats only once readers and revenue justify it. Before you publish, check your employment contract for rules on outside work.

  1. Weeks 1 to 2. Choose the beat, write the point of view sentence and list 50 topics. Write the first offer in one paragraph. Set up the newsletter and a simple subscribe page.
  2. Weeks 3 to 4. Write three issues before announcing anything, so you start with a buffer. Build one lead magnet from your strongest topic.
  3. Weeks 5 to 12. Publish one issue a week and two or three posts a week. Ask for forwards in every issue. Line up one guest appearance or newsletter swap a month.
  4. End of week 12. Review subscribers by source, the issues with the most replies and the questions people asked. Pitch the first offer to the readers who replied most.

By then you'll know whether you enjoy the weekly rhythm and whether readers respond to the beat, which tells you more at this stage than the subscriber count.

Questions people ask

How many subscribers do you need to make money from a newsletter?

There's no fixed number, because it depends on what you sell. At beehiiv's median paid conversion rate of 0.62% and a $10 monthly price, subscriptions need thousands of readers to add up to much. A service or workshop sold to readers who hold budgets can pay from a much smaller list, because one sale is worth hundreds of subscriptions.

Should I start my newsletter on Substack, beehiiv or LinkedIn?

Pick the social platform where your readers already are for discovery, and an email platform such as beehiiv, Substack or Kit for the list you own. For a B2B tech beat that's usually LinkedIn plus one email tool. Compare the tools on subscriber export, referral features and paid options, then commit to one and keep publishing.

Can I write a newsletter about my industry while I'm still employed?

Usually yes, if your contract allows outside work and you keep your employer's confidential information out of it. Read the clauses on outside activities, public statements and intellectual property first. Write from patterns across your career, and don't name your employer or its clients without permission.

How long does it take to grow a newsletter audience?

Longer than most people expect, so plan for at least a year of weekly publishing alongside other income. beehiiv reports that the median newsletter launched on its platform in 2025 earned its first dollar in 66 days, and a full income takes much longer. After 90 days, judge progress by replies, forwards and where subscribers come from, as well as the total.

Should a new newsletter be free or paid?

Start free unless your beat is one where readers already pay for information, because a free list grows faster and gives you people to sell to. beehiiv's 2026 data shows the typical creator launched a paid tier 45 days after starting. For an expert, a free newsletter with a clear service or product offer gives you more ways to earn early on than a paywall.
