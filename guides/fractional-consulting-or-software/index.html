Fractional, consulting or software: which business should you start after tech?

Madalena Rugeroni · Founder of Madgrowth · ex-Google · Updated 17 September 2026 · 10 min read

If you need income within a few months, start with fractional or consulting work. Build software when you can go a year or more without it paying you, or when client work already covers your bills while you build.

The short answer
  • Fractional and consulting work can pay within weeks, because you sell a skill you've already proven to people who already know you.
  • Software usually takes years to reach meaningful revenue: in ChartMogul's 2025 data on 6,525 software companies, 13.4% reached $1M ARR within three years of first charging customers.
  • Services income is capped by your calendar. Productized services and software can grow past your hours, in exchange for more upfront work and more risk.
  • A sensible sequence for many ex tech leaders is services first, then a productized offer, then software for the buyers you already serve.
  • Choose the model whose ordinary week you'd enjoy, because that week is what you'll be doing for years.

What business models can a tech leader start after a corporate career?

There are six realistic models for someone leaving a senior role in tech: a fractional role, consulting or advisory, a productized service, an agency, software (often micro-SaaS) and an audience or media business. They differ in how fast they pay, what they cost to start and what your week looks like.

Quick definitions, so the rest of this guide is precise:

  • A fractional executive is a senior leader who works part-time in an ongoing role for one company, such as head of growth two days a week, usually paid a monthly retainer.
  • Consulting or advisory is paid judgment on a defined problem, sold as a fixed-fee project or as a monthly retainer for access to you.
  • A productized service is a service sold like a product: the same scope, price, timeline and deliverable for every client.
  • An agency is a services business where a team does the delivery and you run sales and the people.
  • Micro-SaaS is a small software business, usually self-funded and run by one to three people, selling a subscription to a narrow niche.
  • An audience or media business earns from attention: a newsletter, a community, courses or sponsorships.

You can combine them, and I cover combinations further down. First, compare them one at a time.

How do fractional, consulting and SaaS compare?

Fractional and consulting work are the fastest and cheapest to start, while software and audience businesses are the slowest to pay and the only ones with no hard ceiling on income. The other models sit in between.

How do I choose between a services business and a software company?

Decide on five things in this order: how long you can go without income, how much risk you can carry, how far past your own hours the income needs to grow, which skills you're willing to learn and what you want an ordinary week to look like. The first two usually rule options out. The last three pick between what's left.

1. Time to first revenue: how long can you go without income?

Your runway is your savings divided by your monthly costs, and it decides more than preference does. If you're leaving a job with less than a year of runway, start with a model that pays in weeks.

Services pay fastest because you're selling work you've already done in a job, to people who've seen you do it. A former VP of product can sell a pricing review to a founder she knows within a month of deciding to.

Software is slower than it looks. Stripe's 2025 review of Atlas startups put the median time from incorporation to first payment at 34 days. Getting from a first payment to a salary takes much longer. ChartMogul's 2025 SaaS Growth Report (6,525 software companies) found that 3.3% reached $1M in annual recurring revenue within a year of first charging customers, 13.4% within three years and 25.1% within five.

Here's what replacing a salary takes in each model. Say you want 10,000€ a month before tax. Illustrative numbers:

  • Fractional: two clients at 5,000€ a month each.
  • Productized service: four fixed-price audits at 2,500€ each, every month.
  • Micro-SaaS at 49€ a month: 205 paying customers, kept after churn.

Two clients is a few good conversations. Two hundred and five subscribers is a marketing system.

2. Capital and risk: what are you putting on the line?

Services risk very little cash, and software risks your time before it risks your money. Each model has its own risk, and it helps to name it before you choose.

  • Fractional and consulting: concentration. With two clients, losing one halves your income overnight.
  • Agency: payroll. You commit to people before the next retainer renews.
  • Software: months of building for demand you haven't confirmed. Hire a developer and it becomes cash too.
  • Audience: a year of publishing with no guarantee the readers will buy anything.

If you're still employed, the risk picture changes. Software and publishing fit around a job more easily, because you can do them in the evenings. Consulting and fractional work need you in meetings during business hours, which is hard to hide from a full-time calendar. Whatever you pick, read your employment contract for clauses on outside work and intellectual property before you start, especially for software.

3. Leverage: how far past your hours does income need to grow?

Leverage is how much more you can earn without working more hours, and services have the least of it. A fractional or consulting business has a ceiling you can calculate in one line.

Say you charge 1,000€ a day and can bill 12 days a month, because the rest goes on selling and admin. Your ceiling is 12,000€ a month. You raise it in two ways. One is to charge for value instead of time, which is the core of Alan Weiss's and Blair Enns's work on pricing expertise. The other is to narrow what you're expert in, which David C. Baker argues is what lets specialists charge more. My guide on how to price fractional and consulting work goes through both.

Productized services add leverage because delivery gets faster every time you repeat it. Software adds the most, since serving one more customer costs little. Every customer still costs something to acquire, and that cost doesn't shrink on its own.

4. Skills: what will you have to get good at?

Every model makes you do one job you probably never did in your corporate role. Pick the one you're most willing to learn.

  • Services: selling yourself in a conversation and saying no to work outside your scope. Blair Enns's Win Without Pitching Manifesto is the best short read on this.
  • Productized services: packaging. Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers is useful on structuring scope and guarantees so a fixed offer sells.
  • Software: distribution and positioning. April Dunford's Obviously Awesome covers positioning, Bob Moesta's work on jobs to be done explains why people switch products, and Madhavan Ramanujam's Monetizing Innovation makes the case for testing price before you build.
  • Agency: hiring and writing delivery down so other people can run it.
  • Audience: publishing on a schedule, for a long time, with opinions.

Engineering leaders can build the product and still find that the hard part of a small software company is getting it in front of buyers. That job lands on you whether you like it or not.

5. Lifestyle: what do you want an ordinary Tuesday to look like?

Picture a normal working day in each model and notice which one you'd look forward to. A fractional Tuesday is back-to-back calls and a team channel that expects replies. A software Tuesday is quiet building, a support ticket and a marketing task you've avoided for a week. An agency Tuesday is mostly other people's questions.

People choose a business model for its best month. You'll spend far more time in its ordinary weeks, so choose the week.

Is fractional work a real business or a job with more bosses?

Fractional work becomes a business when you have a named offer, more than one client and a repeatable way to find the next client. Without those three, it's a part-time job with less security.

The warning signs are easy to spot. Every engagement starts from a blank proposal. Your rate hasn't changed since you started. All your clients came through one former colleague. The fixes are also clear: write one offer with a fixed shape, cap how many clients you take at once, raise your price when your calendar fills and give yourself a way to be found that doesn't depend on luck. My guide on how to go fractional covers the setup step by step.

Should I start with services and build software later?

Yes, if you have less than a year of runway: services pay the bills, and client work shows you which problem is worth building software for. The risk is that client work fills every hour and the product never starts.

Rob Walling's stair step approach is the best-known version of this. You start with a simple one-time product sold through one channel, repeat that until it's reliable, then move to recurring software. For ex tech leaders I'd adapt it slightly:

  1. Services. Sell your judgment to a specific buyer with a specific problem.
  2. Productized service. Once you've solved the same problem three or four times, fix the scope and publish the price.
  3. Software. Automate the part of the productized service you repeat most, and sell it to the same buyers.

Three rules make the sequence work. Keep the same buyer across every step, so your network and reputation carry over. Block build time on your calendar before clients can take it, such as one fixed day a week. And set a written trigger for shifting your time, for example when product revenue covers your fixed costs for three months in a row.

Some hybrids are stable on their own and never need to become software:

  • A fractional role plus a fixed-price audit that brings in the next fractional client.
  • An audience plus a small advisory practice fed by inbound from readers.
  • Consulting plus a micro-SaaS tool sold to the same buyers.

My own portfolio mixes both sides: Madgrowth sells advisory and digital products, and Mazo is a software company. Running the two side by side is possible. It needs the calendar discipline above.

Which business model fits your Builder Archetype?

Your Builder Archetype describes how you like to work, and each of the five archetypes has models that suit it and models that will wear it down. Here's how they match up.

  • The Advisor fits fractional leadership, advisory retainers and a fixed-price teardown. Few clients, each paying properly for judgment. The trap is underpricing until you've rebuilt a job.
  • The Productizer fits productized services, templates and self-serve courses. Revenue grows with copies sold. The trap is shipping several small things and properly selling none of them.
  • The Venture Builder fits micro-SaaS and AI tools in a niche you already know, often funded by consulting at the start. The trap is building for another quarter before talking to a customer.
  • The Orchestrator fits a boutique agency or a done-for-you service run by a small team on your playbook. The trap is building delivery for ten clients while the pipeline holds one.
  • The Broadcaster fits audience-first businesses: a newsletter, cohort programmes and launches, with advisory work fed by inbound. The trap is growing reach with nothing packaged to sell.

Most people have a primary archetype and a secondary pull. An Advisor with some Venture Builder in them might run fractional work for two years and then build a tool for the problem they kept fixing. That's the stair step from the previous section, chosen to suit how you work.

Questions people ask

Can I build a micro-SaaS while working as a fractional executive?

Yes, if you protect the build time before clients claim it, for example one fixed day a week that no client can book. Build for the same kind of company you already serve, so your fractional work doubles as customer research. Set a written trigger for when the software gets more of your week.

How much savings do I need before leaving my job to start consulting?

Work it out as runway: savings divided by your monthly costs. Consulting can pay within weeks, so a shorter runway is workable than for software, as long as you have a few people in your network who already know what you fix. If you have no conversations lined up, start the business alongside your job and leave once the first client signs.

Is a productized service better than a monthly retainer?

A productized service grows further past your hours, because the scope is fixed and delivery gets faster each time. A retainer gives steadier monthly income and deeper client relationships. You can also sell both: a fixed-price audit as the way in, and a retainer for clients who want you to carry out the fix.

Do I need a technical co-founder to start a software company after a non-engineering role?

Not always. You can validate demand and price before writing code, and small tools can be built with AI coding assistants or a freelance developer. If the product is the long-term bet, a technical partner reduces the risk that everything depends on outside help.

What is the difference between a fractional executive and a consultant?

A fractional executive holds an ongoing role inside one company, part-time, and owns outcomes like a team or a number. A consultant is hired to diagnose or fix a defined problem, usually for a set project or an advisory retainer. Fractional work looks like a part-time job, and consulting looks like a series of projects.
