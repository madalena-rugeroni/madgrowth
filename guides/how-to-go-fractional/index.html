How to become a fractional executive

Madalena Rugeroni · Founder of Madgrowth · ex-Google · Updated 17 September 2026 · 10 min read

To become a fractional executive, pick one leadership problem you've solved more than once, package it as a monthly role with a set number of days and named outcomes, and sell it to companies that need that leader before they can justify a full-time hire. Get your first two or three clients from people who already know your work, and start alongside your job only if your contract and your employer's interests allow it.

The short answer
  • Going fractional means holding an ongoing leadership role (CMO, CTO, CPO, VP or head of a function) at a company for part of the week, usually one or two days, for a monthly fee.
  • Fractional leaders get hired by startups and scale-ups at a moment of change, such as a funding round, a senior leader leaving or a board asking for a plan, when the company needs senior judgment before it can afford or attract a full-time executive.
  • A fractional offer should state the days per month, the outcomes you own, a minimum term of at least three months and how many companies you work with at once.
  • The first fractional clients come most easily from your own network: list the people who have seen your work, find the ones whose companies are in a trigger moment and ask for a conversation about that specific problem.
  • Fractional is the wrong model if you want to advise without owning results, or if you want income that can grow past your own hours.

What does fractional mean, and how is it different from interim, consulting or advising?

A fractional executive is a senior leader who holds a real role inside a company, with a title, ownership of a function's results and often a team, for a fixed share of the week and a monthly retainer. You sit in the leadership meetings. You set the plan and answer for the number, on one or two days a week instead of five.

Buyers mix it up with three other models. Two questions separate them: how much of your time does the company get, and who owns the result?

Ownership is the line that matters. If the company expects you to own pipeline, the roadmap or the engineering team, you're fractional, whatever the contract says. If they expect you to tell them what you'd do, you're an advisor, and you should price and scope it that way.

On titles: buyers search for "fractional CMO" or "fractional CTO", so use the title they recognise. Then name the problem you fix, because "fractional CMO" covers brand work for a consumer app and pipeline for B2B SaaS, which are two different jobs.

Who hires a fractional CMO, CTO or CPO?

Fractional leaders are hired by founders and CEOs whose company has outgrown what the founding team can do in a function, but who can't yet justify, afford or attract a full-time executive for it. In tech that's usually a startup or scale-up with some revenue and a function that nobody senior owns. Outside tech, it's often an established small or mid-sized business that needs a technology or product leader for the first time.

What makes them buy now is a trigger. "We should probably get someone" doesn't sign a retainer. Something has to change:

  • A funding round closed and investors expect a plan for the money.
  • A head of marketing, product or engineering left and the gap is showing.
  • A quarter was missed and the board wants to know who owns the fix.
  • The company is about to hire its first full-time executive for the function and doesn't know what good looks like.

The person who signs is usually the CEO or founder, because they own the budget and feel the gap most. When you write your offer, describe the trigger in their words. "We raised in March and nobody owns pipeline" is a sentence a founder says. "End-to-end growth enablement" is a sentence nobody says.

They'll compare you with a full-time hire, with months of recruiting and the risk of choosing the wrong person. Your offer has to look better than that at their stage.

What should you package as a fractional executive?

Package a fixed monthly scope: one problem, a set number of days, the outcomes you own in the first 90 days, a minimum term and a cap on how many companies you take on.

Start from a problem you've solved more than once

Your offer should sit on something you've done at least twice, in a company at a stage close to the buyer's. Built a sales team from founder-led sales to the first five reps, say, or set up product management in a company that had never had it. If you're unsure which is strongest, start with working out what to sell from your tech career before you write any scope.

Write the outcomes first, then the activities

Alex Hormozi's offer thinking is useful here: start from the result the buyer wants, list everything that could stop them getting it, and make your scope the set of things that removes those obstacles. For a fractional role, that gives you outcomes like "a hiring plan and the first two hires made" or "a pipeline report the board trusts". Weekly calls and Slack access go lower down, as delivery details.

Set the days and the boundaries

State the time plainly: one day a week, two days a week, or a number of days a month. Then write the boundaries that protect those days. Which meetings you attend, how fast you reply between days, what's out of scope. Without these, a one-day role turns into three days at the same fee.

Add a minimum term and an exit

Three months is the shortest term I'd accept for a leadership role, because the first month is mostly learning the company. After that, go monthly with a notice period. Also write down how the engagement ends well, for example by handing over to the full-time leader you helped hire.

An illustrative example put together:

  • Role: fractional head of growth for B2B SaaS companies that raised a seed round in the last six months.
  • Time: 2 days a week, with replies within one working day in between.
  • Outcomes in 90 days: one acquisition channel tested with clear numbers, a weekly pipeline report, and a job description plus shortlist for the first marketing hire.
  • Term: 3-month minimum, then monthly with 30 days' notice.
  • Capacity: two companies at a time.

How much should you charge as a fractional leader?

Charge a monthly retainer tied to the days and outcomes in your scope, and check it against two numbers: what the company would pay for a full-time hire, and what you need to earn across the number of clients you can realistically hold. Don't show an hourly rate. It invites the client to count hours instead of results.

Public benchmarks for fractional fees in Europe are thin, and many come from platforms selling fractional services. Interim management has better data. The German interim management association DDIM projects an average interim day rate of 1,317€ for 2026 in its DDIM Marktstudie 2026, averaged across functions and seniority levels. It's a German figure for interim work, so use it as a sanity check for what a senior day costs a company in that market.

Now the maths. Say you sell 2 days a week to each client, roughly 8 days a month, and price the retainer at the equivalent of 1,000€ a day. That's 8,000€ a month per client. Two clients take four days of your week and bring 16,000€ a month before tax and costs, with one day left for selling and admin. A third client doesn't fit. If that ceiling is below what you need, raise the price or change the shape of the offer.

Blair Enns and Alan Weiss both argue for pricing against the value of the result, and fractional work suits that because the buyer has a clear alternative cost. The full method is in how to price fractional and consulting work. For comparison, my own go-to-market advisory for startups is 3,000€ a month with up to four sessions and a three-month minimum: an advisory shape, with less time and less operating ownership than a fractional role.

How do you get your first fractional clients?

Get your first two or three fractional clients from people who have already seen you work, such as former managers and ex-colleagues who now run companies or teams.

1. Write the list

Open a spreadsheet and write down everyone who has seen your work closely enough to describe it. Aim for 50 names. Add their company and whether they could buy or refer.

2. Mark who is near a trigger

Go through the list against the triggers above. Who just raised, lost a leader or opened a job for the role you'd fill? Investors see these moments early across many companies, so give them their own conversation.

3. Send a short, specific message

Name the problem and the stage, and ask one question. An example: "I'm starting to work as a fractional head of growth for seed-stage B2B companies, two days a week, for teams where nobody owns pipeline yet. Is anyone in your portfolio in that spot right now?" A message like that is easy to forward.

4. Make the first call about their problem

Ask what happens if the problem isn't fixed in six months and what they've already tried. Bob Moesta's jobs-to-be-done interviews are a good model: you want the story of what changed and why now. If they need more certainty before a retainer, offer a short paid diagnostic with a fixed fee that turns into the first month if they continue.

5. Publish what you know, once a week

While the network conversations run, write one post a week about the problem you fix: a mistake you've seen or a decision you'd now make differently. It gives your list something to forward and lets people outside your network find you. Fractional marketplaces help once your offer is clear, but they take a cut, so treat them as a second channel.

Can you do fractional work while you're still employed?

You can start fractional work alongside a job if your contract allows it, there's no conflict of interest with your employer and you have the hours to do the role properly. Check all three before you send the first message.

In the EU, Directive (EU) 2019/1152 (Article 9) says employers can't prohibit workers from taking up employment with other employers outside their work schedule, or treat them badly for it. It's written about employment, so self-employed side work may be treated differently. The same article lets member states allow restrictions on objective grounds, including business confidentiality and avoiding conflicts of interest. Each country applies this differently, and your contract may still include exclusivity, intellectual property or non-solicitation clauses. This is general information and no substitute for legal advice: read your contract, and if anything is unclear, pay an employment lawyer in your country for an hour.

In practice, a few rules keep you out of trouble:

  • Don't work for your employer's competitors, customers or suppliers.
  • Don't use company time, company equipment or anything you learned in confidence.
  • Don't approach colleagues or clients from your current job.

Time is the harder constraint. A two-day fractional role doesn't fit next to a full-time job. What does fit is a smaller shape: an advisory retainer of a few hours a month, or a fixed-scope diagnostic delivered over a few weekends. Use those to test the buyer and the price. When one or two clients want a full fractional scope, you have a reason and a date to leave.

When is fractional the wrong fit?

Fractional is the wrong fit when you don't want to own a function's results week to week, or when you want income that grows past your own calendar. Check this before you spend months building a pipeline for a role you'll resent.

  • You like the thinking and dislike operating. Fractional means running a team inside someone else's company, politics included. If you enjoy the hard call more than the weekly operating, an advisory retainer suits you better. The Advisor archetype describes this profile: fewer clients, higher fees for judgment, and a strong tendency to undercharge.
  • You want to build something that runs without you. Fractional income is capped by your days. If you'd rather design the delivery system and hire people to run it, look at a small senior agency or studio. That's the Orchestrator archetype.
  • You want to own a product. If the problem you keep solving could be solved by software, compare the two paths properly in fractional, consulting or software before you commit.
  • You don't want to keep selling. Good fractional roles end when the company hires a full-time leader, so your pipeline never gets to stop.

Questions people ask

How many clients can a fractional executive take on at once?

It depends on how many days each role needs. If each client gets one or two days a week, two or three clients is the practical limit, because you still need time every week to sell and handle admin. Write the cap into your offer so clients know it from the start.

Do I need to set up a company to work as a fractional executive in Europe?

Not always. In many European countries you can start as a self-employed sole trader and set up a company later, once the income justifies the extra cost and admin. Tax, VAT and social security rules differ by country, so talk to a local accountant before you send your first invoice.

Can I be a fractional CTO or CMO if I never held that exact title?

Yes, if you've owned the outcomes the role covers, such as running the engineering team or owning pipeline, at a company close to the buyer's stage. What a buyer checks is whether you've fixed their problem before. Describe that problem and your proof in the offer, and use the title the buyer searches for.

What should I do if a fractional client asks me to join full-time?

Decide your answer before it happens, because it comes up when a role goes well. If you'd say no, offer to help recruit and hand over to the full-time leader, which is a good way to end an engagement. If you might say yes, agree the terms openly and give your other clients proper notice.

Should I join a fractional marketplace to find my first clients?

Start with your network, where people have already seen your work and trust comes faster. Marketplaces are useful once you can state exactly what you sell and to whom. They take a cut of each engagement and expect you to pitch yourself, so treat them as a second channel.
