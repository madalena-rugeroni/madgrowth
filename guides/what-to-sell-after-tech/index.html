What to sell after leaving a tech job

Madalena Rugeroni · Founder of Madgrowth · ex-Google · Updated 17 September 2026 · 9 min read

Sell an outcome you've delivered more than once, to a buyer who already spends money to get it. Write that down as one sentence (who buys, what problem, your proof) and test it with 10 real buyers before you build a website or a product.

The short answer
  • An angle is a specific buyer, the problem they have and your proof that you've fixed it before. It fits in one sentence.
  • You find it in three places: outcomes you've delivered at least three times, problems people already bring to you, and who held the budget when that outcome mattered.
  • One angle can become a fractional role, an advisory offer, a productized service or software. Choose the shape that matches how you like to work.
  • Test it cheaply: name 20 companies that fit, have 10 conversations about how they handled the problem last time, and ask for a small paid engagement.
  • Three traps stall people early: selling your job title, describing a buyer too broad to recognise themselves, and picking buyers who feel the pain but have no budget.

What can you sell after leaving a tech job?

After a career in tech you can sell four things: part of your week in a leadership seat, your judgment, a fixed package of work, or a product. Each one is a different business, with different income and a different kind of week.

A fractional executive is a senior leader who works part time for one or more companies in a defined role (fractional CMO, VP engineering, head of growth), usually on a monthly retainer. An advisor or consultant sells judgment on a specific problem, by project or retainer. A productized service is a fixed scope of work at a fixed price, such as an audit or a four-week sprint. Software and digital products (tools, templates, courses, paid newsletters) sell without your hours attached, and take longer to earn anything.

All four start from the same raw material: an outcome someone will pay for. So find the outcome first and choose the format second. I run both kinds myself. Madgrowth sells advice and AI skills; Mazo is software. The format question gets its own guide: fractional, consulting or software.

How do you find your niche from the career you already have?

Your niche sits where three things overlap: work you've done repeatedly, problems people already ask you about, and buyers who had budget for that result. David C. Baker makes the case for this in The Business of Expertise: expertise comes from seeing the same problem many times, and a narrow focus is what lets you see it often enough to beat a generalist.

Start with an honest inventory of the last 5 to 10 years.

Step 1: list the outcomes you've owned more than once

Write down results you were accountable for, in different companies or teams. Built the first sales team. Moved a product from sales-led to self-serve. Cut infrastructure costs before a fundraise. Took a company into Germany. Rebuilt an onboarding flow that was losing users.

Three times is a useful bar. By the third time you know which parts change from company to company and which don't, and that pattern is what a client pays for. Add a number wherever you can: pipeline, revenue, cost, time, headcount. "Grew pipeline from 2M€ to 8M€ in 18 months at a Series B fintech" (an illustrative example) is proof. "Head of growth" is a title.

Step 2: write down what people already ask you about

Look at the last year of messages, coffee requests and calls from former colleagues. What do they want your help with? Hiring their first engineer, pricing a new plan, fixing a stalled launch, deciding whether to raise.

These requests matter because the problem arrives already worded by someone with the problem. Copy their words exactly. You'll reuse them in your offer.

Step 3: find who paid for that outcome

For each outcome, ask who signed off on the money when it mattered. Alan Weiss calls this person the economic buyer: the one who owns the budget for the problem. They often aren't the person who feels it most. A marketing manager feels the pain of a flat pipeline; the CEO of a 40-person company approves the spend to fix it.

Then check for prior spend. Has this type of company paid an agency, a consultant or a contractor for this before? If yes, a budget line already exists and you're competing for it. If nobody in your target group has ever paid for outside help on this problem, you'd be asking them to create a new line item, and that's a much harder sale.

Step 4: write the angle as one sentence

Put the three pieces together: a specific buyer in a specific moment, the problem in their words, and your proof. For example: "Seed-stage B2B founders who've just raised and have nobody owning pipeline. I've built that function from zero three times." If you can't fill in all three parts, you have a topic, and topics don't get bought.

What does a good angle look like?

A good angle names a buyer you could list 20 companies for, a moment that makes the problem urgent, and proof with numbers in it. Services tend to get bought at moments of change: a funding round, a new VP, a missed quarter, a market launch, a board deadline. Tie your angle to one of those moments and the buyer knows when to call you.

Which kind of business should your angle become?

Pick the business shape by how you want to work, because a single angle can support several shapes. The pipeline angle above could be a fractional seat, a fixed-price audit, a course for first-time growth hires or a reporting tool. The difference is how your week looks and how much of your time each euro costs.

I group these preferences into five Builder Archetypes:

  • Broadcaster: your name is the distribution. Content, courses, community and paid newsletters.
  • Advisor: a few clients who pay properly for judgment. Fractional seats and advisory retainers. More on the Advisor archetype.
  • Productizer: package the work once and sell it many times. Audits, sprints, templates.
  • Venture Builder: the long game. Software or a company that could be worth far more than your hours.
  • Orchestrator: you build the team or system that delivers the work. Agencies and studios.

Your refusals narrow it fast. If you won't be on camera or post publicly, drop the audience businesses. If you don't want client calls most days, services are a bad fit. If you won't build software and have no technical partner, software can wait.

Time matters too. A fixed-scope offer like an audit can fit around a full-time job. A fractional seat usually needs at least a day a week, and a founder who needs you on a Tuesday afternoon. If fractional is where you're heading, read how to go fractional next.

How do you test the angle before you quit your job?

Test an angle with named buyers, conversations about the past and a small paid offer, before you spend anything on a brand, a site or a product. You can do all of it alongside a job in a few weeks of evenings.

Name 20 companies

Write down 20 real companies that fit the buyer in your sentence, with the name of the person who'd approve the spend. If you can't reach 20, the angle is too vague to act on or the market you can reach is too small.

Have 10 conversations about what already happened

Ask people how they handled this problem the last time it came up. What did they try, who did they hire, what did it cost, who decided? Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test is the short guide I'd read first. The core idea: questions about past behaviour get honest answers, while questions about your idea get polite ones.

Leave your pitch out of these calls. Listen for their words, past spend, and whether the problem is on this quarter's list.

Ask for money with a small fixed offer

Once a few conversations confirm the problem, offer something small with a clear scope and a price. Say you offer a two-week pipeline diagnostic for 2,500€, ending in a written plan. A yes gives you revenue and a reference. "Let's talk next quarter" means the urgency isn't there yet. For setting the number, see how to price fractional and consulting work.

Read the evidence honestly

Signs to keep going: people describe the problem before you do, they've paid for outside help on it before, and at least one person agrees to pay. Signs to change the angle: lots of interest and no budget, every conversation turns into free advice, or buyers can't say when they'd need it.

One practical point if you're employed: read your contract for clauses on outside work, conflicts of interest and intellectual property before you take a paid engagement.

What mistakes should you avoid when choosing what to sell?

The first mistake to check for is selling your job title, because a title tells the buyer which seat you sat in and says nothing about what changes for them. The others follow a similar logic.

  • Selling the title. "Fractional CMO" is a category buyers understand, which is useful. On its own it gives nobody a reason to pick you over the next profile. Attach the focus: fractional CMO for B2B SaaS companies entering the DACH market.
  • Too broad. "I help B2B companies grow" includes almost everyone, so nobody recognises themselves in it. If a buyer can't tell within one sentence that you mean them, they move on.
  • Buyers with no budget. Pre-revenue founders, individual employees paying from their own pocket, and your old team (instead of their boss) all feel the pain. Few of them can sign off on a retainer.
  • Picking the most interesting angle over the one with proof. Start with the one you can prove and move towards the other once clients trust you.
  • Building before selling. Months on a platform, a brand or a course before one conversation with a buyer. The test above takes weeks and costs almost nothing.
  • Pricing from your last salary. Dividing your old salary into a day rate ignores the time you'll spend selling, the weeks between clients and what the problem costs the client.

Should you pick one angle or keep your options open?

Pick one angle for the next 90 days and park the rest on a list. Each angle needs its own list of buyers, its own message and its own conversations, so testing three at once leaves you with too little evidence on any of them.

Committing is less final than it feels. Narrow positioning is easy to widen once people refer you; a vague one is hard to sharpen later, because nobody remembers what you do. April Dunford makes a related point about positioning in Obviously Awesome: start from the customers who get the most value from you, and describe yourself in terms they already understand.

A practical order: write three candidate angles, rank them by strength of proof and how easily the buyer can pay, and test the top one. If the evidence is weak after 10 conversations, move to number two.

Questions people ask

Can I start a consulting business while still employed in tech?

Yes, if your employment contract allows it. Read the clauses on outside work, conflicts of interest and intellectual property before you take a paid engagement. A small fixed-scope offer, such as an audit, fits around a full-time job more easily than a weekly fractional seat.

What if my experience feels too generic to find a niche?

Start with outcomes. List results you delivered more than once, with a number and a context, and the moment a company needed them: after a round, a new VP, a missed quarter. The overlap of a repeated outcome and a buyer with budget is usually narrower than your CV suggests.

Should I niche by industry or by function?

Choose whichever gives you the stronger proof and a buyer list you can actually name. A function (pricing, RevOps, security certification) travels across industries, while an industry focus (fintech, healthtech) makes referrals easier. Combining one of each, such as RevOps for fintech scale-ups, gives you the sharpest angle.

What is the difference between a fractional executive and a consultant?

A fractional executive holds an ongoing leadership role part time, owns results and usually manages people or budget, paid by monthly retainer. A consultant or advisor is brought in for a specific problem or project and gives recommendations or a plan. You can start with one and add the other later.

How do I know if anyone will pay for my expertise?

Ask the people you want to sell to how they solved the problem last time and what they spent on it. If they've paid an agency, a contractor or a consultant before, a budget exists. Then offer a small paid engagement at a fixed price. A yes is the strongest evidence you can get.
